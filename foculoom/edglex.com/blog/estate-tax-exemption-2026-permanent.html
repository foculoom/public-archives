The Federal Estate Tax Exemption Is Now $15 Million — and Permanent. What That Changes.

By Edglex  ·  June 8, 2026

For nearly a decade, estate planners operated under a structural uncertainty: the doubled estate tax exemption created by the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act was set to expire at the end of 2025. Without congressional action, the exemption would have reverted from roughly $14 million back to approximately $7 million — cutting the federal threshold in half. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act resolved that uncertainty. It made the elevated exemption permanent and, with 2026 inflation adjustments applied, set the basic exclusion amount at $15 million per decedent.

That figure is not a new policy. It is the inflation-adjusted continuation of the TCJA's elevated exemption, now without a sunset date. What changed is the planning horizon. Prior to the OBBB, estate plans drafted in 2023 or 2024 frequently included contingencies for the 2025 sunset. Those contingencies no longer apply. The legal and financial logic of estate planning in 2026 is built on a stable $15 million base — until Congress changes it again.

How the Federal Estate Tax Works

The federal estate tax is a transfer tax imposed on the taxable estate of a decedent — the total value of property transferred at death, less allowable deductions (marital deduction, charitable deduction, debts, expenses). The tax applies only to the amount of the taxable estate that exceeds the applicable exclusion amount. For 2026, that exclusion is $15 million per decedent.

The Unified Credit and Applicable Exclusion Amount

The "applicable exclusion amount" under IRC § 2010 is the combined value that can be transferred free of federal estate and gift tax over a taxpayer's lifetime and at death. It is "unified" because the same lifetime exemption pool covers both taxable gifts made during life and the taxable estate at death. A taxpayer who makes $5 million in taxable gifts during their lifetime reduces their remaining estate tax exclusion by $5 million. For 2026, the applicable exclusion is $15,000,000 per person, as set by IR-2025-103 (Oct. 9, 2025) implementing Revenue Procedure 2025-32.

At a 40% top estate tax rate, the tax on amounts above the exclusion can be significant. A $20 million estate with a $15 million exclusion leaves $5 million potentially subject to tax — a federal estate tax liability of up to $2 million at the 40% rate (before any deductions, credits, or state-level considerations). At prior exemption levels of approximately $7 million, that same $20 million estate would have had $13 million exposed to tax — a dramatically different picture.

What the Permanence of the Elevated Exemption Changes

The 2025 Sunset That Did Not Happen

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 (Pub. L. 115-97) roughly doubled the estate and gift tax exemption from approximately $5.49 million (2017) to $11.18 million (2018), with inflation adjustments through 2025. The TCJA included a sunset provision: after December 31, 2025, the exemption would revert to pre-TCJA levels (approximately $7 million after inflation adjustment). The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Pub. L. 119-21) repealed the sunset. The elevated exemption is now permanent law. Estate plans built around the sunset contingency — including irrevocable trusts funded specifically to take advantage of the elevated exemption before the sunset — should be reviewed by counsel to assess whether their structure is still optimal under permanent law.

Portability: The Surviving-Spouse Rule

Under IRC § 2010(c)(5), a surviving spouse may inherit any unused portion of the deceased spouse's applicable exclusion amount — a concept called "portability." If a decedent dies in 2026 with a $15 million exclusion and a taxable estate of $10 million, the remaining $5 million exclusion can be ported to the surviving spouse — but only if the executor timely files a federal estate tax return (Form 706) and makes the portability election, even if no estate tax is owed. Portability is not automatic. Failure to file Form 706 within the required time period means the deceased spouse's unused exclusion is lost. IRS has provided relief procedures for late portability elections under certain circumstances; consult current IRS guidance before concluding that a missed deadline is irreversible.

The Annual Gift Tax Exclusion — A Separate Planning Tool

The $15 million lifetime exemption is separate from and in addition to the annual gift tax exclusion under IRC § 2503(b). For 2026, the annual exclusion remains $19,000 per recipient per year. Gifts at or below $19,000 per recipient do not reduce the donor's $15 million lifetime exemption and do not require filing a gift tax return (Form 709).

Gift-Splitting for Married Couples

Married couples may elect to split gifts under IRC § 2513, treating a gift from one spouse as made one-half by each spouse. A couple can together give $38,000 per recipient per year (2 × $19,000) free of gift tax reporting and without touching the lifetime exemption — using a single Form 709 to make the split election. The election must cover all gifts made to all third parties during the calendar year once elected; it cannot be applied selectively to individual recipients. For families with multiple children or grandchildren, annual gifting at the exclusion limit can transfer substantial wealth over time without using any portion of the lifetime exemption.

The annual exclusion is a use-it-or-lose-it tool: unused annual exclusion amounts do not carry forward to future years. A person who could have given $19,000 to a beneficiary in 2025 but did not cannot give $38,000 to that beneficiary in 2026 and treat $19,000 as attributable to 2025.

State Estate Taxes Are a Separate Calculation

The federal estate tax exemption does not govern state-level estate or inheritance taxes, which exist in a number of states. Some states have their own estate taxes with exemption amounts well below $15 million. Others have inheritance taxes that apply to beneficiaries rather than estates. Several have no estate or inheritance tax at all. A decedent's state of domicile and the location of real property determine which state regimes apply; a $15 million federal exemption does not eliminate state estate tax exposure in states with lower thresholds.

"The federal estate tax exemption is now $15 million per person and permanent. But the planning decisions that surround it — portability elections, gifting strategy, trust structure, and state tax exposure — still require careful execution."

What This Means for Estate Plans Drafted Under the Sunset

Many estate plans drafted between 2018 and 2025 assumed the TCJA exemption would sunset. These plans sometimes included provisions for irrevocable gifts or trust contributions timed to occur before the end of 2025, to lock in the elevated exemption. Some of those plans executed as designed; others were not yet executed when the OBBB passed. Regardless of the execution status, estate plans that were structured around the sunset assumption — including AB trusts, spousal lifetime access trusts, and other irrevocable arrangements — should be reviewed by qualified estate planning counsel now that the elevated exemption is permanent. The tax position may be unchanged, improved, or unnecessarily complex, depending on how each plan was drafted.

This is legal information, not legal advice. Estate planning is highly individual, and the interaction of federal and state law varies by jurisdiction. Consult a qualified estate planning attorney and tax professional before making planning decisions based on the current exemption level.
