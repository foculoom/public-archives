The New $6,000 Senior Deduction: How It Stacks With the Tax Benefits Older Americans Already Have

By Edglex  ·  June 5, 2026

Taxpayers 65 and older already receive an additional standard deduction under existing federal law — a modest bump to the standard deduction that has existed for decades under IRC § 63(f). The One Big Beautiful Bill Act added something different: a separate $6,000 deduction per qualifying individual, available whether or not you itemize, on top of the § 63(f) amount. The two provisions coexist; they are not the same thing.

That distinction matters because many taxpayers — and some tax software — treat the two provisions as interchangeable. They are not. The existing § 63(f) additional standard deduction is a relatively small inflation-adjusted amount. The new OBBB senior deduction is $6,000 per qualifying person, with its own eligibility criteria, its own phaseout calculation, and its own form (Schedule 1-A). Understanding both is necessary to claim what you are actually entitled to.

The Two Deductions for Seniors: What Each One Is

Existing Additional Standard Deduction (IRC § 63(f))

Under IRC § 63(f), taxpayers who are 65 or older (or legally blind) are entitled to an additional amount added to their standard deduction. This amount is inflation-adjusted annually. For tax year 2025, it was $1,600 for single filers 65 or older ($3,200 if both spouses qualify and file jointly, using the 2025 base figures). This deduction automatically increases the standard deduction; it is not a separate line item on Schedule 1-A. It is built into the standard deduction table in the Form 1040 instructions and applies only to taxpayers who take the standard deduction, not itemizers.

New OBBB Enhanced Senior Deduction (OBBB Section 70103)

Effective for tax years 2025 through 2028, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act added a separate $6,000 deduction per qualifying individual age 65 or older. For a married couple where both spouses qualify, the combined deduction is $12,000. This deduction is claimed on Schedule 1-A and available to both taxpayers who take the standard deduction and those who itemize. Per IRS FS-2026-04, Schedule 1-A deductions reduce taxable income — they do not reduce adjusted gross income (AGI). It is subject to its own income phaseout that does not apply to the § 63(f) amount.

A taxpayer who takes the standard deduction, is 65 or older, and has MAGI below the phaseout threshold may be entitled to both: the § 63(f) additional standard deduction increment and the new $6,000 OBBB senior deduction. The two are additive, not mutually exclusive. A taxpayer who itemizes receives only the new $6,000 OBBB deduction (since itemizers do not take the standard deduction and therefore do not receive the § 63(f) increment).

Eligibility for the OBBB Senior Deduction

Age Requirement

The deduction is available to taxpayers who are age 65 or older during the tax year. The IRS applies the same age-determination rule used elsewhere in the Code: a taxpayer is considered to have reached age 65 on the day before their 65th birthday. A taxpayer who turns 65 on January 1, 2026 is considered to have been 65 on December 31, 2025, for tax-year-2025 purposes. Verify the applicable IRS guidance for the year you are filing to confirm age computation rules.

Income Phaseout — MAGI Thresholds

The $6,000 deduction phases out for taxpayers with modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) above $75,000 for single filers (or married filing separately) and $150,000 for married couples filing jointly. At sufficiently high MAGI, the deduction is eliminated entirely. The phase-down calculation is performed on Schedule 1-A. Taxpayers near the phaseout threshold should compute their MAGI carefully — Social Security income, required minimum distributions, and investment income all affect MAGI and can push a taxpayer into or past the phaseout range.

Effective Period

The new senior deduction is effective for tax years 2025 through 2028. It applies on the 2025 return filed in 2026, and through the 2028 return filed in 2029. Unless Congress extends the provision, it sunsets after tax year 2028. The existing § 63(f) additional standard deduction for seniors is permanent law and is not affected by the sunset.

Why the Phaseout Threshold Is Lower Than Other OBBB Deductions

The OBBB tips and overtime deductions phase out above $150,000 MAGI for single filers. The car loan interest deduction phases out above $100,000 MAGI for single filers. The senior deduction phases out above $75,000 MAGI for single filers — the lowest threshold of the four new Schedule 1-A taxable-income deductions.

That threshold interacts with Social Security income in ways that can be counterintuitive. Up to 85% of Social Security benefits are includible in gross income for taxpayers above certain combined-income levels. A retiree with a modest Social Security benefit, pension, and some investment income may have a higher MAGI than expected — potentially pushing them above the $75,000 threshold even if they do not consider themselves high-income.

"A retiree with Social Security, a pension, and investment income may hit the phaseout threshold without realizing it. MAGI for this deduction includes income that is often overlooked."

Common MAGI inclusions that taxpayers may undercount: taxable Social Security benefits, traditional IRA distributions, required minimum distributions (RMDs) from qualified plans, and net investment income from dividends and capital gains. If your gross income before these items is near the threshold, computing MAGI precisely before claiming the deduction is essential.

Interaction With the Standard Deduction and Itemizing

The OBBB senior deduction is structurally different from the § 63(f) additional standard deduction in one important way: it is available to itemizers. Taxpayers who deduct state and local taxes, mortgage interest, charitable contributions, or large medical expenses on Schedule A do not receive the standard deduction at all — which means they forgo the § 63(f) increment. But they can still claim the $6,000 OBBB senior deduction, because it is a Schedule 1-A deduction from taxable income, not a component of the standard deduction.

Because the $6,000 OBBB senior deduction reduces taxable income rather than adjusted gross income, it does not affect AGI-based calculations such as the 7.5% AGI floor for the medical expense deduction (IRC § 213), the Social Security benefit income inclusion formula, or other AGI-based phaseouts. Those calculations remain anchored to AGI, which is computed before Schedule 1-A deductions are applied. The senior deduction's benefit operates downstream of those thresholds, on the taxable income line.

Documentation and Filing

The OBBB senior deduction is claimed on Schedule 1-A, Part V (the applicable part as designated in the instructions for the year you are filing). No separate proof of age is required at filing — but age is established from the taxpayer's date of birth on the return. The IRS may request verification during correspondence examination. Keep a copy of your identification and any documentation supporting your date of birth.

The IRS published a dedicated eligibility checklist for the enhanced senior deduction at irs.gov/newsroom/check-your-eligibility-for-the-new-enhanced-deduction-for-seniors and additional filing-season resources for seniors at irs.gov/newsroom/2026-filing-season-updates-and-resources-for-seniors. Review both before filing.
