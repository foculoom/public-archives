The 20% Business Income Deduction Was Temporary. The OBBB Just Made IRC § 199A Permanent.

By Edglex  ·  June 9, 2026

The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act created a deduction that applied to roughly 26 million Americans who own pass-through businesses — sole proprietors, partners, S-corp shareholders, and certain trust and estate beneficiaries. It allows them to deduct up to 20 percent of their qualified business income. And it was always temporary. Section 199A was written with a sunset: after December 31, 2025, it would expire. For eight years, every tax plan involving a pass-through business had to account for the possibility that this deduction would disappear.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act removed that contingency. The OBBB made the § 199A deduction permanent, effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. That change does not alter how the deduction is calculated. It does not change who qualifies or what the limitations are. What it removes is the planning uncertainty — the sunset is gone, and the deduction is now expected to be available indefinitely unless Congress acts to change it again.

What the § 199A Deduction Is

Qualified Business Income Deduction — Basic Structure

Under IRC § 199A, individuals (and certain trusts and estates) may deduct up to 20 percent of qualified business income (QBI) from a qualified trade or business conducted as a sole proprietorship, partnership, S-corporation, or through a trust or estate. The deduction is a below-the-line deduction from taxable income — it does not reduce adjusted gross income (AGI), but it does reduce taxable income directly and is available to both itemizers and taxpayers who take the standard deduction. It is claimed on Form 8995 (simplified) or Form 8995-A (when the income threshold is exceeded or special rules apply, such as the SSTB phaseout or the W-2 wage and UBIA limitation). The deduction reduces federal income tax; it does not reduce self-employment tax or net investment income tax.

The deduction is not available to C-corporations. It applies exclusively to pass-through income — income that is reported on the owner's individual return rather than taxed at the entity level. A business owner who operates through an S-corp or partnership pays the entity-level tax rate applicable to their entity type (none, for most S-corps and partnerships), then includes their share of business income on their personal return, where the § 199A deduction may apply.

Qualified Business Income: What It Includes and Excludes

QBI is net ordinary income from a qualified trade or business conducted within the United States. Not all pass-through income qualifies. Several categories are specifically excluded from QBI under the statute:

What Is Not QBI

Under IRC § 199A(c)(4), the following types of income are excluded from QBI regardless of their source:

Capital gains and losses — net capital gains are not QBI, even if generated within a pass-through business.

Dividends and dividend equivalents — qualified dividend income from a pass-through investment is not QBI.

Interest income not allocable to the trade or business — bank interest earned by a business entity is generally not QBI.

Reasonable compensation paid to an S-corp shareholder-employee — W-2 wages paid by an S-corp to its owner are not included in QBI; only the remaining business profit flowing through to the shareholder qualifies.

Guaranteed payments to partners — guaranteed payments received from a partnership are not QBI to the recipient partner.

What remains after these exclusions — the net ordinary operating income of the qualified business, allocated to the taxpayer — is the QBI base. The 20% deduction is applied to that figure, subject to the limitations below.

The Two Major Limitations

The § 199A deduction is subject to two limitations that apply above an inflation-adjusted income threshold. For taxpayers below the threshold, neither limitation applies and the deduction is straightforwardly 20% of QBI (not to exceed 20% of taxable income minus net capital gains). For taxpayers above the threshold, the limitations reduce — and in some cases eliminate — the deduction.

Limitation 1: W-2 Wages and Qualified Property (Above Threshold)

For taxpayers with taxable income above the applicable threshold, the § 199A deduction for each qualified business is limited to the greater of: (a) 50 percent of W-2 wages paid by that business, or (b) 25 percent of W-2 wages plus 2.5 percent of the unadjusted basis immediately after acquisition (UBIA) of qualified property held by the business. This limitation incentivizes businesses that employ workers or hold significant depreciable assets. A sole proprietor with no employees and no depreciable property may have their deduction reduced to zero by this limit once their income exceeds the threshold — even if they have substantial QBI. The thresholds are inflation-adjusted annually under Rev. Proc. 2025-32; verify the applicable amounts for the tax year you are filing in the current Form 8995-A instructions.

Limitation 2: Specified Service Trades or Businesses (SSTBs)

Certain service businesses — grouped as "specified service trades or businesses" (SSTBs) — are subject to a separate limitation that phases out the deduction entirely at higher income levels. SSTBs include businesses in the fields of health, law, accounting, actuarial science, performing arts, consulting, athletics, financial services, brokerage services, and any business where the principal asset is the reputation or skill of one or more of its employees or owners. If your business is an SSTB and your taxable income exceeds the upper phaseout boundary, your § 199A deduction for that business is zero regardless of QBI, wages, or property. Between the lower and upper phaseout boundary, the deduction phases out proportionally. Non-SSTB businesses — manufacturing, retail, real estate, most trade businesses — are not subject to the SSTB limitation. Engineering and architecture are explicitly excluded from SSTB status under the statute.

What the Permanence of § 199A Means for Planning

For eight years, every entity-structure analysis for a new or existing business included a variable: "the § 199A deduction expires in 2025 unless extended." That variable is gone. The deduction is now permanent, and business owners can plan around it without a sunset horizon.

"The § 199A deduction is not a loophole. It is a statutory provision that applies to roughly a quarter of all U.S. taxpayers with pass-through income. The planning opportunity is not in gaming the rule — it is in understanding precisely which businesses qualify, which income counts, and where the limits reduce the benefit."

For S-corp shareholders, one persistent planning consideration involves reasonable compensation: S-corp owners who pay themselves below-market salaries to maximize the QBI base (since only remaining profit — not W-2 wages — is QBI) may face IRS scrutiny. The IRS can reclassify inadequate compensation as S-corp distributions, which increases W-2 wages, increases FICA exposure, and may affect the deduction calculation. Reasonable compensation must be paid for services actually rendered to the business, and the amount must reflect what an arm's-length employer would pay for those services in that industry and market.

How to Claim the Deduction

Taxpayers with taxable income at or below the applicable threshold who have QBI from a qualified business use Form 8995 (the simplified form), provided other eligibility conditions are met. Taxpayers whose taxable income exceeds the threshold, or who have SSTB income or W-2 wage and UBIA limitations to compute, use Form 8995-A, which walks through the W-2 wage limitation and SSTB phaseout computations. Having multiple qualified businesses alone does not require Form 8995-A; it is the income threshold and special-rule triggers that determine which form applies — verify the current-year Form 8995 instructions before filing. Both forms flow to the deduction line on Form 1040, reducing taxable income. The deduction does not require a separate statement or additional documentation at filing — but the underlying QBI calculation must be supportable on audit. Maintain records of business income, W-2 wages paid, and the unadjusted basis of qualified property held by each business.

This is legal information, not legal advice. The § 199A deduction involves complex fact-specific determinations — including whether an activity constitutes a qualified trade or business, whether compensation is reasonable, and how the W-2 wage and SSTB limitations apply to your situation. Consult a qualified tax professional before making entity structure or compensation decisions based on this deduction.
