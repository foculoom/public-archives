The 2026 Standard Deduction Is $32,200 for Joint Filers. Here Is What Else Changed.

By Edglex  ·  June 6, 2026

Every October, the IRS publishes an annual inflation adjustment notice that resets more than 60 tax figures for the coming year. The numbers land in a revenue procedure, get summarized in a press release, and — for most taxpayers — immediately disappear into the background. They are not the kind of news that generates headlines. But they are the kind of numbers that determine whether you under-withhold for the year, whether your retirement contribution room is larger than last year, and whether certain thresholds you have been planning around have shifted.

For 2026, that release is IR-2025-103 (October 9, 2025), implementing Revenue Procedure 2025-32. The standard deduction increases came in two layers this year: the usual inflation adjustment, plus the effect of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which altered the base amounts for the 2025 and 2026 standard deductions in addition to its other new provisions. Here is what changed, what it means, and which figures actually matter to most filers.

The Standard Deduction for 2026

Standard Deduction Amounts — Tax Year 2026

These figures apply to returns for the 2026 tax year (generally filed in 2027). The amounts reflect both the regular inflation adjustment and the OBBB amendments to the standard deduction base:

Single filers and married filing separately: $16,100

Married filing jointly and qualifying surviving spouses: $32,200

Head of household: $24,150

These are the amounts before any additional standard deduction for age (65+) or blindness under IRC § 63(f). Additional standard deduction increments for seniors and the blind are added on top of these base figures and are separately inflation-adjusted.

For context: the standard deduction for tax year 2025 was $15,750 for single filers and $31,500 for married filing jointly — amounts also set by the OBBB as a one-time adjustment to the 2025 base, independent of inflation indexing. The 2026 figures represent the inflation-adjusted continuation of those OBBB-amended bases.

The standard deduction governs whether most taxpayers benefit from itemizing. If your total deductible expenses — mortgage interest, state and local taxes up to the applicable cap, charitable contributions, medical expenses above the AGI floor — do not exceed the applicable standard deduction for your filing status, taking the standard deduction produces a larger benefit. For a married couple with $29,000 in itemizable expenses, the standard deduction of $32,200 is worth more. That comparison shifts every year as both the standard deduction and itemizable expenses change.

Other Key Figures for 2026 From Revenue Procedure 2025-32

The standard deduction is the most widely cited figure, but it is not the only one that moves. Several other inflation-adjusted amounts from IR-2025-103 affect a large number of taxpayers.

Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)

The maximum EITC for tax year 2026 is $8,231 for qualifying taxpayers with three or more qualifying children (up from $8,046 for 2025). The EITC phases out above income thresholds that vary by filing status and number of qualifying children; see the Form 1040 instructions or IRS Publication 596 for the applicable tables. The EITC is refundable — meaning eligible taxpayers receive it even if they owe no federal income tax.

Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT) Exemption

The AMT exemption for 2026 is $90,100 for unmarried individuals (phasing out beginning at $500,000) and $140,200 for married couples filing jointly (phasing out beginning at $1,000,000). Taxpayers with higher incomes, large numbers of preference items, or significant incentive stock option exercises should compute their tentative minimum tax alongside regular tax. The AMT exemption under IRC § 55 prevents ordinary income from being captured by the parallel tax system up to these thresholds.

Annual Gift Tax Exclusion

The annual exclusion for gifts remains at $19,000 per recipient per year for tax year 2026 — unchanged from 2025. Gifts at or below this amount do not require a gift tax return (Form 709) and do not reduce the taxpayer's lifetime estate and gift tax exemption under IRC § 2503(b). Married couples can elect gift-splitting to double the per-recipient exclusion to $38,000 using a single Form 709.

Estate Tax Basic Exclusion Amount

For estates of decedents who die during 2026, the basic exclusion amount is $15,000,000 (up from $13,990,000 for decedents dying in 2025). The One Big Beautiful Bill Act made the elevated estate tax exemption permanent; it no longer faces the post-2025 sunset that was written into the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act. See also: our dedicated post on the estate tax exclusion.

Foreign Earned Income Exclusion

For tax year 2026, the foreign earned income exclusion under IRC § 911 is $132,900 (up from $130,000 for 2025). U.S. citizens and resident aliens living and working abroad who meet either the bona fide residence test or the physical presence test may exclude up to this amount of foreign-source earned income from U.S. federal income tax. The exclusion is claimed on Form 2555. Housing cost exclusions and deductions under § 911 are subject to separate limits and formulas.

Health Flexible Spending Arrangement (FSA) Limit

For plan years beginning in 2026, the limit on voluntary employee salary reductions for contributions to health FSAs under IRC § 125 increases to $3,400 (up $100 from 2025). The maximum FSA carryover of unused amounts to the following year rises to $680 (up $20 from 2025). Health FSA contributions reduce taxable wages — the tax benefit is the combined federal income tax rate, FICA, and state income tax rate on the amount contributed. For a taxpayer in the 22% federal bracket, contributing $3,400 saves approximately $750 in federal income tax alone, before FICA and state tax savings.

What the Marginal Rate Thresholds Mean

The marginal tax rate brackets are also inflation-adjusted for 2026. The 22% bracket applies to income over $50,400 for single filers ($100,800 for married filing jointly). The 24% bracket begins at $105,700 ($211,400 for joint filers). The top rate of 37% applies to income over $640,600 for single filers ($768,700 for joint filers).

These thresholds shift the decision point for tax planning strategies that depend on bracket position: Roth conversions, timing of capital gains realization, additional retirement contributions, and deferred compensation decisions. A taxpayer close to a bracket boundary in 2025 may find themselves in a lower bracket in 2026 due to the inflation adjustment, even without any change in income.

"Inflation adjustments are not newsworthy. They happen every year. But they are the foundation of every calculation you make about withholding, bracket planning, and contribution limits. Ignoring them is how people over-withhold and under-contribute for years at a time."

Where to Find the Full Table

Revenue Procedure 2025-32 contains the complete table of all 60-plus adjusted figures for tax year 2026. The IRS press release IR-2025-103 provides a summary of the most commonly used figures. Both are available at irs.gov. The full revenue procedure is the authoritative source; use it for planning purposes rather than relying on a third-party summary, which may omit less-common provisions that are relevant to your situation.
