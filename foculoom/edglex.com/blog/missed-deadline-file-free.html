Missed April 15? You Can Still File Free and Claim Overlooked Tax Breaks.

By Edglex  ·  June 3, 2026

Most people treat April 15 like a cliff. If they missed the filing deadline, they assume the window is closed and whatever deductions, credits, or refund they might have claimed are gone. The IRS is now explicitly saying that assumption is wrong for many taxpayers: in a June 2 newsroom release, the agency reminded filers that they can still submit their 2025 federal return for free through IRS Free File.

That matters because the people most likely to miss a filing date are often the same people most likely to miss obscure tax benefits. If you skipped filing because you thought it would be expensive, confusing, or pointless, the late-file window can still be worth real money — especially if you qualify for deductions that did not exist in prior years or credits you did not realize you were eligible to claim.

What Changed on June 2

IRS reminder: missed deadline does not always mean missed refund

In IR-2026-71 (June 2, 2026), the IRS states that taxpayers who missed the filing deadline can still file their 2025 federal return at no cost using IRS Free File. The same release says IRS Free File remains available through Oct. 15. The IRS also notes that eligible taxpayers, including people who do not usually file, may still qualify for overlooked credits or refunds.

This is not a loophole in the sense of a secret carveout. It is a process gap: a legal right that exists in public guidance but is easy to miss if you stop at "I missed the deadline." If you are due a refund, filing late can still recover money. If you owe tax, late filing can still reduce additional penalties compared with not filing at all.

Who Can Use Free File Right Now

On IRS.gov, the Free File page says taxpayers with adjusted gross income of $89,000 or less can use guided partner software for free federal filing, and often free state filing depending on partner rules. Taxpayers above that threshold can still use Free File Fillable Forms to e-file a federal return without software interview flows.

IRS also emphasizes a practical detail many people miss: you must start through IRS.gov to get program protections and the correct free workflow. Going straight to a commercial landing page can route you to paid products that look similar but are not the same IRS Free File channel.

Why This Is Especially Relevant for 2025 Returns

Tax year 2025 introduced new "Additional Deductions" reporting through Schedule 1-A. That includes newer deduction categories that many filers did not see in prior years. If you delayed filing, you are not just filing late — you are filing in a year with expanded deduction complexity and additional documentation requirements.

Late filing can still preserve overlooked deductions

A missed April deadline does not eliminate your ability to file a return and claim deductions you qualify for. But delay increases risk: records get harder to collect, and people default to filing quickly without checking newer forms like Schedule 1-A. Filing now — with records in hand — is usually better than filing never.

The core pattern is simple: legal provisions reward completed paperwork, not awareness. You do not get a deduction because it exists in statute. You get it because you file a valid return, on a valid form, with sufficient support.

"The deduction is not lost because you missed April 15. It is lost if you never file, or file without the records and form entries needed to claim it."

Checklist Before You File Late

If you are filing after the deadline, move deliberately. Start with document collection, then use IRS Free File through IRS.gov, then validate that your return includes all forms tied to your actual facts. The IRS Free File page specifically points taxpayers to gather income statements, records supporting deductions and credits, and prior-year AGI or self-select PIN for electronic signature validation.

For many taxpayers, the highest-value step is not speed — it is completeness. If you had tipped income, overtime premium, a new financed vehicle, or age-based eligibility for additional deductions, those are not assumptions software can infer without accurate inputs.

The Real "Loophole" Is Procedural

There is no secret law hidden in a private database. The opportunity is procedural: the IRS has left a free filing door open through Oct. 15 and publicly acknowledged that late filers may still recover credits or refunds they overlooked.

People who act on that guidance can still convert a "missed deadline" story into a "claimed what I legally qualified for" outcome. People who do nothing usually lose by default.
