The Car Loan Interest Deduction Is New in 2026. Here's What to Know Before You File.

By Edglex  ·  June 2, 2026

Corrections and updates — 2026-07-12
Corrected the characterization of Schedule 1-A deductions. Per IRS FS-2026-04, these deductions reduce taxable income, not adjusted gross income (AGI). Removed language describing them as "above-the-line" AGI deductions. The structural description — that they are available to taxpayers who take the standard deduction — is unaffected.
Source: IRS FS-2026-04, Schedule 1-A, Additional Deductions: What to know about the new form.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act created four new deductions that taxpayers can now claim on their federal returns using Schedule 1-A. Three of them — tips, overtime pay, and the enhanced senior deduction — received at least some public attention when the legislation passed. The fourth received almost none: a deduction of up to $10,000 for qualified interest paid on a new passenger vehicle loan.

It applies to personal vehicle purchases, it is subject to income phaseouts, and it is available whether you itemize or take the standard deduction — the same structural feature that makes the tipped income and overtime deductions unusually accessible. But unlike those provisions, which target specific categories of workers, the car loan interest deduction applies to anyone who financed the purchase of a qualifying new vehicle. That is a large population of taxpayers who will not find this provision unless they go looking.

Here is what the deduction is, who qualifies, and what you need to know before you claim it.

What the Deduction Covers

New Passenger Vehicle Loan Interest Deduction

Taxpayers may deduct up to $10,000 in qualified interest paid during the tax year on a loan for a new passenger vehicle. Per IRS FS-2026-04, the deduction reduces taxable income (not adjusted gross income) and is available to taxpayers who take the standard deduction — it does not require itemizing. It is claimed on Schedule 1-A with your Form 1040. The deduction applies to personal-use vehicles only; business vehicles are subject to separate deduction rules under IRC § 179 and the listed property provisions. The vehicle must be new — purchases of used vehicles, even from a dealer, do not qualify. Income-based phaseouts apply; consult current IRS guidance for the applicable thresholds, which may be adjusted for inflation.

The conceptual structure is similar to the mortgage interest deduction, but with critical differences. Mortgage interest is an itemized deduction; this one is claimed on Schedule 1-A as a taxable-income deduction. The mortgage interest deduction has no income ceiling in most cases; this one phases out. And the mortgage interest deduction has existed in federal law since 1913; this one is new for tax year 2025, effective on returns filed in 2026.

The Core Eligibility Requirements

There are six questions that determine whether you can claim this deduction. All conditions must be met. The conditions below reflect IRS guidance published at irs.gov/newsroom/one-big-beautiful-bill-provisions-individuals-and-workers and the Schedule 1-A instructions.

1. Is the vehicle a qualifying vehicle?

The deduction applies only to a qualified vehicle — defined by IRS as a car, minivan, van, SUV, pickup truck, or motorcycle that meets both of the following conditions:

a) Gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR) under 14,000 pounds. Vehicles at or above 14,000 lbs GVWR do not qualify, regardless of vehicle type or use.

b) Final assembly in the United States. The vehicle must have undergone final assembly in the U.S. To verify, check: the vehicle label at the dealership, the vehicle identification number (VIN), or the NHTSA VIN Decoder (nhtsa.dot.gov). This is a hard eligibility requirement — assembly location cannot be waived.

The vehicle must also be originally used by the taxpayer — the IRS uses the phrase "originally used," meaning the taxpayer is the first owner placing the vehicle in service. Certified pre-owned, used vehicles, and demo units with prior service history do not qualify.

2. Is it financed with a qualifying loan?

The deduction is for loan interest paid, not for the vehicle purchase itself. A cash purchase generates no deductible interest. Per IRS guidance, the loan must meet all three of these requirements:

a) Originated after December 31, 2024. Loans on vehicles purchased before 2025 do not qualify, regardless of when the vehicle was purchased.

b) Secured by a first lien on the vehicle. The loan must be a first-lien security interest on the purchased vehicle. Second-lien or unsecured personal loans used to finance a vehicle purchase do not qualify.

c) Proceeds used to purchase the vehicle for personal use. The loan proceeds must have been used to purchase the vehicle — not for cash-out or other purposes.

Lease payments do not qualify under any circumstances. Refinancing note: If a qualifying vehicle loan is later refinanced, IRS guidance states that interest paid on the refinanced loan is generally eligible for the deduction — the refinancing does not automatically disqualify the interest. Principal payments are never deductible regardless of refinancing.

3. Is it a personal-use vehicle?

The deduction is for personal use only. If you use the vehicle for both business and personal purposes, the interest must be allocated between business and personal use; only the personal-use portion qualifies for this deduction. Business-use portions may be deductible under different provisions. A vehicle used exclusively for commuting to work is typically classified as personal-use; vehicles used to transport clients, carry equipment to job sites, or make deliveries as part of a trade or business involve a more complex analysis.

4. Do the income phaseouts affect you?

The deduction phases out at higher modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) levels. IRS has published the specific thresholds: the deduction phases out for taxpayers with MAGI over $100,000 (single filers) or $200,000 (married filing jointly). At sufficiently high income, the deduction is eliminated entirely. These thresholds are set in the statute; consult current IRS guidance and the Schedule 1-A instructions for the applicable phase-down calculation and any inflation adjustments in effect for the tax year you are filing.

5. Have you reported the VIN on your return?

IRS requires that the vehicle identification number (VIN) of the purchased vehicle be reported on Schedule 1-A for every year you claim the deduction. This is a documentation condition — failing to include the VIN can invalidate the deduction on audit. Your lender is also required by law to file an information return with the IRS and provide you a statement showing total qualified interest paid during the taxable year; retain this statement with your tax records.

⚠️ Verify with current IRS guidance before filing

The conditions above reflect IRS guidance current as of June 2026 (FS-2026-04 and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act provisions page). IRS may issue additional guidance, corrections, or clarifications. Before filing, verify the complete eligibility checklist — including assembly, GVWR, lien, and documentation requirements — at irs.gov/newsroom/one-big-beautiful-bill-provisions-individuals-and-workers and the Schedule 1-A instructions. Consult a qualified tax professional before claiming this deduction, particularly if your vehicle's assembly location is unclear, your loan has been refinanced, or you use the vehicle for any business purpose.

Why This Provision Gets Missed

The tipped income and overtime exclusions are targeted at workers in specific industries. Tax preparers and employers in those industries have at least some incentive to surface them. The senior deduction is targeted at taxpayers 65 and older, a demographic that receives regular outreach from financial advisers and senior-focused tax assistance programs.

The car loan interest deduction has no such natural constituency. It is not industry-specific. It applies broadly to anyone who financed a new personal vehicle — a category that cuts across every income level and demographic that falls under the phaseout threshold. There is no trade association filing notices about it, no HR department sending reminders, and no automatic prompt in standard tax software that asks "did you buy a new car on credit this year?"

"There is no automatic prompt in standard tax software that asks whether you bought a new car on credit this year. That means the deduction exists whether or not you find it — and the IRS will not tell you that you missed it."

The result is the same information asymmetry that governs every legal provision: it is in the public record, it is available to anyone who qualifies, and the vast majority of eligible taxpayers will not claim it because they do not know it exists. The provision does not discriminate based on sophistication. It simply rewards whoever finds it.

What to Do If You Purchased a New Vehicle in 2025 or 2026

If you purchased a new vehicle through a financed loan in tax year 2025 or 2026 and used it for personal purposes, the first step is to determine whether your income falls below the phaseout thresholds. If it does, the deduction is worth calculating. Gather your loan documentation — specifically the annual interest paid figure, which your lender is required to report to you — and review the IRS guidance on Schedule 1-A to confirm the current-year eligibility requirements.

Two caveats worth noting: the deduction applies to interest you actually paid during the tax year, not interest that accrued but was not paid. And for vehicles purchased late in the year, the amount of interest paid through December 31 may be modest. The full deduction benefit typically accrues over the full first year of the loan when interest front-loading is highest.

This deduction does not require a tax professional to claim — it is a standard Schedule 1-A item — but the eligibility analysis for mixed-use vehicles, and the phaseout calculation at higher income levels, benefits from professional review.
