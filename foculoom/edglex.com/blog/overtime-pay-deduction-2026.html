No Tax on Overtime: How the New Premium-Pay Deduction Actually Works

By Edglex  ·  May 31, 2026

The "no tax on overtime" provision from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act has a structural detail that almost every news summary leaves out. The deduction is not for all overtime pay. It is specifically for the premium portion of qualified overtime compensation — the extra increment above your regular rate of pay. In plain terms, it is the "half" in "time-and-a-half," not the whole amount.

That distinction is not a footnote. It determines exactly how much of your overtime check is deductible, and whether you are calculating the deduction correctly. A worker earning $30 per hour in overtime does not deduct $30 per hour. They deduct the $10 per hour premium — the amount above their regular $20 per hour rate — multiplied by overtime hours worked, up to the annual cap.

Here is how the deduction works, who qualifies, and where the compliance pitfalls are.

What the IRS Says the Deduction Covers

Qualified Overtime Compensation — Premium Only

According to IRS guidance, individuals may deduct qualified overtime compensation — specifically, "the pay that exceeds their regular rate of pay, generally, the 'half' portion of 'time-and-a-half' compensation" — required by the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) and reported on a Form W-2, Form 1099, or other specified statement. The deduction does not apply to the entire overtime paycheck. It applies only to the increment above the worker's regular hourly rate.

The FLSA requires employers to pay covered, non-exempt employees at least one and a half times their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. The new deduction targets exactly that premium — the "and a half" component that exists because federal law requires it. The regular wages earned during those overtime hours are not deductible under this provision; they are ordinary W-2 wages subject to normal tax rules.

The Cap and Phaseout Thresholds

Annual Deduction Limits

Maximum deduction: $12,500 per year for single filers. $25,000 per year for married couples filing jointly.

Phaseout: The deduction phases out for taxpayers with modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) above $150,000 for single filers, or $300,000 for joint filers. At sufficiently high income, the deduction is eliminated entirely. Consult the Schedule 1-A instructions for the phase-down calculation applicable to your tax year.

Available to both itemizers and non-itemizers: The deduction is claimed on Schedule 1-A and does not require itemizing on Schedule A. Per IRS FS-2026-04, Schedule 1-A deductions reduce taxable income (not adjusted gross income) — they flow to Form 1040 after the AGI line and operate as deductions in computing taxable income.

The cap of $12,500 represents a practical ceiling. A worker earning $25 per hour with a $12.50 premium would exhaust the deduction after 1,000 hours of premium pay — roughly equivalent to 25 hours of overtime per week for a full year. Most workers will not approach the cap. But higher-earning hourly workers, or those with significant overtime schedules, may hit it.

Who Qualifies — and Who Does Not

The deduction is available to workers who receive FLSA-required overtime, plus equivalent overtime pay under comparable state or federal law, as reported on the required tax forms. There are several categories of workers for whom eligibility is more nuanced.

FLSA-Exempt Employees

The FLSA exempts certain categories of employees from its overtime requirements — primarily salaried workers in executive, administrative, professional, or certain highly compensated positions. If your overtime is a discretionary bonus, a contractual premium, or a payment voluntarily made by your employer above and beyond FLSA requirements rather than FLSA-mandated time-and-a-half, the deduction may not apply. IRS guidance states that the qualifying overtime compensation must be required by the FLSA and reported on the specified forms. Discretionary or non-FLSA overtime pay does not clearly qualify under the plain text of the provision as described by the IRS.

Gig Workers and Self-Employed Individuals

The IRS describes the deduction as applying to overtime compensation "reported on a Form W-2, Form 1099, or other specified statement furnished to the individual." Self-employed individuals and independent contractors typically do not receive FLSA-mandated overtime because the FLSA covers employees, not independent contractors. The deduction's applicability to gig workers depends on whether their compensation arrangement produces income that falls within IRS guidance on qualified overtime. If you are self-employed and believe you have qualifying overtime compensation, confirm eligibility with a tax professional before claiming the deduction.

Effective Period

The deduction is effective for qualified overtime compensation earned in tax years 2025 through 2028. It applies to the 2025 return filed in 2026, and through the 2028 return. Unless Congress acts to extend it, the provision sunsets after tax year 2028.

How the Calculation Works in Practice

The deduction is not simply "overtime pay" from your W-2. Your W-2 box 1 wages include both your regular earnings and your overtime earnings combined. The premium portion of overtime is not separately broken out on a standard W-2. You must calculate it based on your regular rate of pay and the number of overtime hours worked.

Example Calculation (Illustrative Only)

A worker earns $24 per hour regular rate. For hours over 40 per week, the employer pays $36 per hour (time-and-a-half). The overtime premium is $12 per hour ($36 minus $24). If the worker logs 800 overtime hours during the year, the total premium is $9,600 ($12 × 800). That $9,600 is the deductible amount — assuming MAGI is below the phaseout threshold. The regular $24-per-hour portion of those same overtime hours remains ordinary W-2 income. Always verify your calculation against the Schedule 1-A instructions for the year you are filing.

The IRS requires that the qualifying overtime compensation be reported on the appropriate forms. Retain your pay stubs, employer statements, or any documentation that separates regular and overtime hours and rates for each pay period. If your employer's W-2 or Form 1099 does not separately identify the overtime premium, you will need your own records to substantiate the deduction on audit.

"The deduction is not for all overtime. It is for the premium — the extra amount above your regular rate that federal law requires your employer to pay you. That structural detail changes the math significantly."

Filing the Deduction

The qualified overtime deduction is claimed on Schedule 1-A, the new additional deductions form released by the IRS in 2026. Schedule 1-A consolidates the new taxable-income deductions created by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act: tipped income, overtime compensation, car loan interest, and the senior deduction. Per IRS FS-2026-04, these deductions reduce taxable income — they do not reduce adjusted gross income (AGI). The overtime deduction appears in Part III of Schedule 1-A. The phase-down calculation for taxpayers near or above the MAGI threshold is performed on the same form.

The IRS's newsroom guidance for the overtime deduction references Tax Tip 2026-06 (January 26, 2026) and the OBBB provisions page as the controlling sources of filing guidance. Check irs.gov for updated instructions before filing.
