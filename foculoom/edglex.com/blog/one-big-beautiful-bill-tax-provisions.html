The One Big Beautiful Bill Created New Tax Provisions. Here's What They Are.

By Edglex  ·  June 1, 2026

Corrections and updates — 2026-07-12
Corrected the characterization of Schedule 1-A deductions — per IRS FS-2026-04 they reduce taxable income, not adjusted gross income (AGI). Removed "above-the-line" descriptions. Updated tipped-income deduction: the IRS has issued final regulations (IR-2026-49, April 10, 2026) defining eligible occupations and qualified tips, replacing prior "expected to define" language. Qualified the overtime premium estimation method.
Sources: IRS FS-2026-04; IR-2026-49.

The reconciliation bill that passed Congress in 2025 — officially the One Big Beautiful Bill Act — is mostly remembered for what it cut. What receives far less attention is what it created: a set of new deductions and exclusions that are sitting in the tax code right now, available to real people who qualify for them, and almost entirely unknown outside of professional tax circles.

These aren't exotic arrangements. They aren't offshore structures. They are statutory provisions — written into federal law, effective this year — that create real tax advantages for specific categories of workers, families, and retirees. The same dynamic that governs every legal loophole applies here: the provision exists whether you know about it or not. The question is whether you find it.

Here are five provisions created or modified by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act that are worth understanding before you file.

Five New Provisions to Know

1. Tipped Income Deduction (Up to $25,000)

For tax years 2025 through 2028, eligible workers can deduct up to $25,000 of cash tips from their federal taxable income (the same ceiling applies to married couples filing jointly — there is no doubled MFJ amount). The deduction applies to workers in industries where tipping is customary. The IRS's final regulations (IR-2026-49, April 10, 2026) identify more than 70 eligible occupations across eight categories, including food service, hospitality, and personal care. Per IRS FS-2026-04, this deduction reduces taxable income (not adjusted gross income) and is available to workers who do not itemize. The phaseout begins at $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income (single) or $300,000 (married filing jointly). For a full-time server earning $18,000 in tips annually and not currently itemizing, this provision alone could reduce federal taxable income by the full amount — an outcome they would never encounter through the standard tax-preparation process unless someone surfaced it for them.

2. Overtime Pay Premium Exclusion (Up to $12,500)

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act provides a federal deduction for the overtime premium — the extra half-pay that makes overtime "time and a half" — for FLSA-qualifying workers, for tax years 2025 through 2028. Only the premium portion qualifies, not all compensation paid for overtime hours. Up to $12,500 per year (single) or $25,000 (married filing jointly) of that premium may be deducted from federal taxable income. If a W-2 does not separate overtime premium from base pay, the Schedule 1-A instructions describe an estimation method: divide total overtime compensation by three to approximate the deductible premium — this estimate assumes the regular rate equals the base rate and depends on your specific overtime hours; verify against your actual pay records. The exclusion phases out beginning at the same modified AGI thresholds as the tipped income deduction. A warehouse employee earning $20/hour who regularly works overtime could easily generate $4,000 or more in overtime premium annually — that premium portion, previously fully taxable as ordinary income, is now largely shielded. The base wages paid for those overtime hours remain taxable.

3. New Senior Deduction ($6,000 Per Person)

Taxpayers age 65 and older receive an additional deduction of $6,000 per qualifying person, available for tax years 2025 through 2028. Per IRS FS-2026-04, this Schedule 1-A deduction reduces taxable income (not adjusted gross income). A married couple where both spouses are 65 or older can claim a combined $12,000 additional deduction. The phaseout begins at $75,000 of modified adjusted gross income for single filers and $150,000 for joint filers, reducing by $1 for every $10 of income over the threshold. For seniors living on fixed income who do not itemize, this deduction represents a meaningful reduction in taxable income that the existing standard deduction structure did not previously provide. It stacks on top of the existing higher standard deduction already available to taxpayers over 65 under IRC § 63(f).

4. SALT Deduction Cap Raised to $40,400

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 capped the state and local tax (SALT) deduction at $10,000 per household — a limit that fell heavily on homeowners in high-tax states like California, New York, and New Jersey who previously deducted far more. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act raises this cap to $40,400 for single filers (indexed for inflation) and the same amount for married filing jointly filers, with a phaseout beginning at $500,000 of modified adjusted gross income. For joint filers with combined state income tax and property tax payments that exceed $10,000 — common in California and New York — the math under the new cap is materially different from 2024. Whether the higher SALT deduction now makes itemizing more favorable than the standard deduction depends on the full picture of a household's deductions, but for homeowners in high-tax states with significant property tax bills, this is a provision that warrants recalculation.

5. 529 Plan K–12 Contribution Limit Doubled

Section 529 education savings plans allow contributions to grow tax-free and be withdrawn tax-free for qualified education expenses. Since 2017, qualified expenses have included K–12 tuition at private or religious schools, subject to a $10,000 annual limit per beneficiary. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act doubles this limit to $20,000 per beneficiary per year for K–12 expenses. For families already contributing to 529 plans for college savings, this change may expand the range of tax-efficient strategies for private school tuition costs — particularly in states that offer their own 529 contribution deductions, where the state benefit can compound the federal advantage. State tax treatment of 529 distributions varies and must be analyzed separately under each applicable state's law.

Why These Provisions Won't Find You On Their Own

None of the five provisions above are secret. They are in the public record, in the statutory text, and in guidance that the IRS is in the process of publishing. But knowing that a provision exists is different from knowing that it applies to you.

Consider the tipped income deduction. A server who earns $20,000 in tips this year and files a return using standard tax software will not automatically see a prompt asking whether they have tip income they want to deduct. Standard software flows are designed around common deductions — mortgage interest, charitable contributions, retirement contributions. A new statutory provision targeting a specific category of worker requires someone to know it exists, know the applicable eligibility criteria, and actively claim it.

The same problem applies to the overtime exclusion. A worker who received $15,000 in overtime pay in 2025 would ordinarily pay federal income tax on all of it. Under the new exclusion, up to $12,500 of the overtime premium portion of that amount is shielded — but only if the worker knows to claim it, correctly calculates the premium component, and understands how the phaseout thresholds interact with their total income. The IRS will not proactively send a letter to every overtime worker explaining that their 2025 taxes just changed.

The information asymmetry problem that governs legal knowledge in general governs new legislative provisions in particular. The closer you are to the professional networks where tax law is discussed — the more likely you are to have heard about this. Everyone else has to find it for themselves.

What to Do Next

If any of the five provisions above potentially apply to your situation, the immediate step is documentation. The tipped income deduction requires records of tip amounts received. The overtime exclusion requires documentation that overtime pay qualifies under FLSA definitions. The senior deduction requires confirming your AGI relative to the phaseout thresholds.

For each provision, the questions worth answering before the filing deadline are: (1) do I qualify based on the statutory definition? (2) how do the phaseout thresholds interact with my total income? (3) does claiming this provision change whether I should itemize or take the standard deduction?

These are answerable questions. They require reading the statute and running the numbers. But for most people, the first barrier is simply knowing that the question exists.
